Why Your AI Drafts Sound Like AI (And the Three Prompts That Fix It)

19 July 2026 · 5 min read

You read the draft. Every fact is right. The structure is fine. Nothing is wrong, exactly.

And yet you wouldn't send it.

Something in it reads as machine-written, and if you can feel that, so can your client. In a profession where your written word is the product, that matters more than it does almost anywhere else.

Here's what's actually happening, and how to fix it in about ninety seconds.

Why it happens

Large language models are trained to sound helpful, agreeable and complete. Left alone they pad. They hedge. They reach for the impressive word over the plain one, and they explain what they're about to say before they say it.

Legal writing rewards the exact opposite. Precision. Brevity. No decoration. Say the position, say the next step, stop.

So the model isn't broken. It's doing what it was built to do. It's just optimising for the wrong thing, and nobody told it otherwise.

The three-second test

Before anything leaves your firm, scan for these. Two or more and it reads as machine-written.

  • Em dashes. AI loves them. Most lawyers rarely use them. It's the single fastest tell.
  • "It is important to note that..." or any sentence that announces itself before saying anything.
  • The rule of three. "Clear, concise, and compelling." The model can't resist a tricolon.
  • Hedging stacked on hedging. "It may potentially be advisable to consider..."

The banned list

Delete these on sight. None of them survive in precise legal writing.

Words: delve, tapestry, testament, underscore, crucial, pivotal, navigate (as in "navigate the complexities"), landscape, realm, robust, comprehensive, myriad, plethora, seamless, unlock, leverage as a verb, utilise, facilitate, foster, holistic, meticulous, intricate, paramount, elevate, streamline, empower, resonate, bespoke.

Openers: "I hope this email finds you well." "In today's fast-paced legal environment." "In an era of..."

Closers: "By doing so, you can..." "Ultimately, the key is..." "Please do not hesitate to reach out."

Structural tells: bullet points where prose belongs, a closing paragraph that repeats what you just said, and every paragraph running to the same length.

Three prompts to copy and paste

1. The client update

Act as a partner at an Australian law firm writing to a commercial client. Draft a client update in dry, objective, precise language. Short sentences. No emotive adjectives. No introductory filler. Never open with "I hope this email finds you well." Do not use em dashes. State the current position, the next step, and the date it is required by. Australian English and Australian spelling. Facts to include: [paste your facts]

2. The de-slop pass

Use this on any draft the model has already produced.

Rewrite the text below to remove AI tells. Delete these words entirely: delve, tapestry, testament, underscore, crucial, pivotal, robust, comprehensive, navigate, landscape, realm, myriad, seamless. Remove all em dashes. Delete any sentence beginning with "It is important to note" or "In today's". Keep every fact and every legal qualification exactly as written. Do not add anything new. Return only the rewritten text. Text: [paste the draft]

3. The voice match

This is the one almost nobody uses, and it's the one that actually works.

Below are three emails I have written to clients. Study my sentence length, my level of formality, and how I open and close. Do not comment on them. [paste three of your own emails] Now draft the following in that same voice, matching my formality exactly. Do not use any word I have not used in those examples. The email to draft: [describe it]

Generic prompts produce generic writing. Hand it your own writing as the reference and it stops guessing at what a lawyer sounds like.

The part nobody tells you

Those three prompts will fix your tone today. Use them. They work, and they're yours, no email address required.

But look at what you've just signed up for.

Every email now takes a trip to another window, a paste in, a tweak, a paste back, and a read-through to check it hasn't invented a date or a defined term. Call it ten to fifteen minutes.

Across five fee earners doing that a few times a day, that's hours every week. You're paying qualified people to act as a copy-paste layer between two browser tabs.

That's the real problem, and it's worth being honest about it: the tone was never the hard part. A prompt fixes tone. Nothing on this page fixes the time.
